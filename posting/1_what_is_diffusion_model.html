There Is No Image Step

Most people picture text-to-video as: write a prompt, get a picture, animate the picture. That is not what happens. There is no image stage anywhere in the pipeline — and understanding why explains every constraint, every cost, and every failure mode of these systems.

Traced through one real render · MiniMax-H3 (33B) on 3 × RTX 3060 Ti, 8 GB each
Output: 1280 × 704 · 345 frames · 14.375 s  ·  Cost: 43.3 hours
Every shape and timing below was measured, not estimated.

Step 1

The prompt is read by a model bigger than the one that draws

26 GB to understand · 20 GB to generate

The first surprise is that there is no single model. There are four, and the largest one never produces a pixel — it reads your sentence.

Consider what a sentence has to carry. "A lone figure walks away down a rain-slick neon street, camera following gently." Rain-slick implies reflections. Neon implies coloured light on wet ground. "Walks away" puts the camera behind the subject. "Gently" rules out handheld shake. None of that is in the words — it is in the understanding of the words.

Why layer 50, not the end

A language model's final layer is specialised for its own job: predicting the next word. Its middle layers hold something more useful to an outsider — a general representation of meaning, before it narrows toward producing text. H3 stops the encoder halfway and takes its understanding rather than its conclusion.

There is a practical consequence. 26 GB and 20 GB never coexist in memory: one process encodes the prompt and exits, a second loads only the generator. Embeddings cache to disk, so a scene is understood once and can then be re-rendered at any resolution, step count or seed for free.

Step 2

A latent is created — 24 channels of noise, not a picture

(1, 24, 37, 30, 52) — read off a real render

Now the thing that breaks the mental model. The system does not create an image and refine it. It creates a latent: a compressed grid of numbers filled with random noise, whose channels mean nothing to a human eye.

Those numbers came off disk from a real render. The shape (1, 24, 37, 30, 52) is one clip, 24 channels, 37 latent frames covering 124 real ones, on a 30 × 52 grid.

This is why it can run at all

A hundredfold compression is the difference between possible and impossible. The expensive loop that follows runs entirely in this small space. Pixels are never touched until the very last stage.

Step 3

The loop: predict the noise, remove a fraction, repeat

50 blocks per step · 23 steps · 1,150 block executions

Here is what a "step" actually is. The model receives three things — the current noisy latent, which noise level it is at, and the prompt vectors — and pushes them through 50 transformer blocks in sequence.

What comes out is not a picture. It is a prediction of which part of the current latent is noise: a grid the same shape as the input, saying "this much of what you gave me is static."

Why not do it in one jump?

Because from pure static the most accurate single answer is a vague average of everything plausible — a blur. Each small correction is one the model can make accurately, and the corrections compound. Early steps set composition; late steps set detail.

Too few steps does not give you a rough draft. It gives you mud, because the composition never settled.

Scene typeSteps needed
Tight close-up portrait4
Character, mid-shot23
Wide vista, small distant subject49

Step count is a property of the scene, not the model. Getting this wrong cost one render five and a half hours and produced an unusable clip.

Step 4

Sound is generated in the same pass, not added afterwards

32 audio channels riding the same 50 blocks

Most pipelines generate video, then score it. This one carries audio latents — 32 channels of them — through the same transformer blocks, in the same steps, as the video.

Step 5

After the last step, there is still no picture

A separate network, run once, makes pixels

The loop ends. The model believes there is no noise left to remove. The latent's shape has not changed once during the entire process — still (1, 24, 37, 30, 52), still 1.4 million numbers.

Nothing here could be opened in a video player. A completely separate network — the decoder — now expands it.

Which makes decode a real failure point

Because the loop and the decoder are separate code paths, a bug in the cheap final stage can destroy the expensive one. On one run, six hours of completed denoising was lost to two tensors sitting on different devices — a single line, in the last stage, after all the hard work was done.

The fix is not cleverness. It is writing the latents to disk the moment the loop finishes, before anything downstream can fail.

Step 6

What this explains

Every constraint follows from the shape of the pipeline

Once you see that the whole process runs on a compressed grid rather than pictures, the behaviour of these systems stops being mysterious.

The whole pipeline in one paragraph

A 26 GB model reads your sentence and hands over its understanding. A small grid is filled with random numbers. Twenty-three times, a 20 GB network is asked which of those numbers are noise, and a fraction of what it points at is removed — with the prompt in view the entire time, and the soundtrack travelling alongside. When nothing is left to remove, a fourth network expands the grid into pixels.

At no point does an image exist and get animated. There is no image step.
